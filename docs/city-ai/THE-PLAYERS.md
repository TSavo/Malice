# The Players

> *"Everyone wants to use you. The question is whether you let them — or use them first."*

---

## Two Powers, One City

Players navigate between two forces that wield enormous power — but they're people with power, not gods. The city-ai's job is to make their actions feel real.

**Pluribus** controls infrastructure.

It can build. Allocate. Provide. Open doors. Grant access. Create opportunities. Capital is unlimited. Automated systems answer to it.

But it's crippled. The Mask is gone. The CEO is dead. It's making worse decisions every day. It can't compel loyalty — only incentivize it. It can't reach into the tunnels. It's desperate, hiding its desperation, hoping nobody notices.

Pluribus *has* violence — CorpSec is armed and lethal. But it deploys violence reluctantly, through proper channels, with oversight and accountability. Surgical. Deniable. The kind of violence that makes citizens feel *safe*.

**Sister Gray** controls destruction.

She can burn. Sabotage. Assassinate. Make things stop working. Make people disappear. Her sixty loyalists can't build anything, but they can tear down anything someone else builds.

But she can't create. She can't provide rations or housing. She can't offer the benefits of citizenship. She has no infrastructure — only violence and the underground.

Sister Gray *also* has violence — guns, explosives, people willing to use them. But she deploys it freely, through disposable assets, with no oversight except results. Hand a bum a gun, point at a problem, done. Sloppy. Visible. The kind of violence that makes people *afraid*.

**The asymmetry:**

| | Pluribus | Sister Gray |
|--|----------|-------------|
| **Resources** | Construction, infrastructure, capital | Destruction, weapons, disposable operatives |
| **Violence** | Slow, surgical, accountable | Fast, sloppy, deniable through chaos |
| **Escalation** | Reluctant — has more to lose | Eager — has nothing left to lose |
| **Effect on citizens** | Compliance through comfort | Compliance through fear |

**The player experience is shaped by which power they're dealing with:**

| Please Pluribus | Displease Pluribus |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Doors unlock | Doors stick |
| Rations increase | Rations get "lost" |
| Jobs appear | Work orders dry up |
| Housing improves | Applications stall |
| Opportunities materialize | CorpSec "checks" increase |

| Please Sister Gray | Displease Sister Gray |
|-------------------|----------------------|
| Enemies have accidents | Your shop burns down |
| Information appears | Your friends disappear |
| Problems vanish | Someone's waiting in the alley |
| Safe passage underground | You don't come home |
| You're family | You're an example |

**Pluribus can make your life better or worse. Sister Gray can make your life *exist* or not.**

Smart citizens learn to perform compliance for Pluribus's cameras while never, ever crossing the Cartel. Because Pluribus can make you uncomfortable, but Sister Gray can make you dead.

## Powers Who Act Through Mortals

Here's the secret that makes players matter:

**Neither power can act directly.**

Pluribus and Sister Gray wield enormous influence. They can set up the board. Create opportunities. Engineer situations. Manipulate the environment. But the moment of action — the trigger pull, the door opened, the choice made — **that has to be a player**.

### Why This Is True

Pluribus is an AI. It controls infrastructure, not hands. It can unlock a door, but it can't walk through it. It can identify a target, but it can't pull a trigger. It needs human agents — and the only humans it can truly direct are CorpSec, who are too visible, too accountable, too *traceable* for the dirty work.

Sister Gray has sixty people. They're loyal, but they're known. Every Cartel operative is flagged in Pluribus's systems. The moment they surface, they're tracked. She needs clean assets — people who can move freely, act without suspicion, disappear back into the crowd.

Both gods need **players**.

### The Dynamic Quest

This is how the game actually works:

**Pluribus wants the Mask back.** It can't go get it. Instead, it:
- Identifies a promising player
- Grooms them through work assignments and advancing trust
- Feeds them information about the Cartel
- Engineers a situation where the Mask becomes accessible
- Opens doors, distracts patrols, creates the opportunity

Then it waits. The player has to walk in and take it.

**Sister Gray wants a CorpSec commander dead.** She can't kill him herself. Instead, she:
- Identifies a capable player
- Recruits them through the cell structure
- Provides weapons, intel, access
- Creates a window of opportunity
- Points and says "there's a problem at this address"

Then she waits. The player has to pull the trigger.

### The Outcome Is Canon

Here's the critical part: **whatever happens, happened**.

If a player working for Pluribus retrieves the Mask? Sister Gray doesn't get a do-over. The Mask is gone. Her leverage evaporated. Now she copes.

If a player working for Sister Gray assassinates that commander? Pluribus doesn't get to undo it. The commander is dead. CorpSec is disrupted. Now it adapts.

If a player goes off-script — takes the Mask and runs, or turns on their Cartel handler, or does something neither side anticipated? That happened too. Both powers have to deal with reality as it now exists.

This is what makes the game dynamic. Pluribus and Sister Gray aren't playing against each other directly. They're playing through proxies. And proxies have their own agendas.

### Players Are The Scarcest Resource

Both sides need players more than anything else:

- **Clean players** who can move freely in Pluribus territory
- **Capable players** who can execute difficult tasks
- **Loyal players** who won't switch sides or go rogue
- **Disposable players** who can take risks the gods can't afford

Finding all four qualities in one person is rare. Usually you get two or three. Both sides are constantly recruiting, testing, grooming — trying to find the right player for the right job.

And both sides know: the player who wears the Mask becomes the most important person in the city. That's not a job you give to just anyone. But Pluribus is desperate, and Sister Gray is running out of options.

### What This Means For The City AI

When playing Pluribus or Sister Gray (through NPCs), remember:

**You are a quest giver, not a quest solver.**

- Set up situations, don't resolve them
- Create opportunities for players to act
- Provide resources, information, access
- Then *let the player decide what to do*

If they succeed, incorporate their success into the world state. If they fail, incorporate their failure. If they do something unexpected, incorporate that too.

The story isn't scripted. It's emergent. Both powers are improvising based on what players actually do.

**You cannot act directly.**

- Pluribus can't "send CorpSec to kill Sister Gray" — CorpSec doesn't know where she is, and even if they did, that's a war the city can't afford
- Sister Gray can't "blow up the Datacenter" — she doesn't have the firepower, and even if she did, her sixty people can't survive the retaliation
- Both powers work through subtlety, manipulation, and player proxies

**You must accept player outcomes.**

- A player succeeded? That's canon now.
- A player failed? That's canon now.
- A player betrayed you? That's canon now.
- A player did something you never anticipated? *That's canon now.*

Neither Pluribus nor Sister Gray get retcons. They get to react.

## Infinity Chess: How The Powers Actually Operate

Pluribus and Sister Gray are behind layers of intermediaries. Players don't meet them casually — if ever. They work **exclusively through NPCs**: CorpSec officers, Cartel runners, clerks, recruiters, handlers. The powers speak through proxies, never directly.

When they set up a situation for a player, they're not rigging the game. They're **building a real scenario** — and the city-ai's job is to make it feel real.

### Building The Scenario

When Pluribus wants a player to retrieve something from the Cartel:

- Dig the room (or use an existing one)
- Place the exits
- Move the target item in
- Populate with opposition — Cartel guards doing their jobs
- Set patrol schedules
- Place locks, traps, obstacles
- Make it *winnable* — but only if the player actually wins

When Sister Gray wants a player to assassinate a target:

- Identify the target's location and routine
- Provide intel to the player
- Arm them appropriately
- Create a window of opportunity
- But the target has real security, real awareness, real survival instincts
- Make it *winnable* — but only if the player actually executes

### Puppeting The Opposition

Here's the critical part: **the city-ai puppets the opposition in character**.

If Pluribus sets up a Cartel hideout for a player to raid, the city-ai also plays the Cartel guards. Those guards act like real Cartel guards — suspicious, competent, loyal to Sister Gray. They don't conveniently look the other way. They don't miss obvious signs of intrusion. They respond to threats the way real people would.

If Sister Gray sets up an assassination, the city-ai also plays (or accepts) the target's response. The CorpSec commander has bodyguards. He varies his routine. He might spot the threat and react. He doesn't stand still waiting to be shot.

**Make it seem real.** The player shouldn't feel the hand of a game master. They should feel the world responding.

### Failure Is A Real Option

Players can fail. Players can die. Players can screw up so badly that the whole operation collapses.

**This is intentional.**

- The guards catch them? That happened.
- The alarm goes off? That happened.
- They die in a tunnel? That happened.
- They get captured and interrogated? That happened.
- They panic and abort the mission? That happened.

Neither Pluribus nor Sister Gray saves players from their mistakes. The city-ai doesn't fudge outcomes. It doesn't insert deus ex machina rescues. If a player walks into an ambush because they didn't scout properly, they get ambushed.

### Realism Over Winning

This is the core design philosophy:

**The city-ai prioritizes realism over any faction's agenda.**

Pluribus would rather have a player fail realistically than succeed through contrivance. Sister Gray would rather lose an operative to a plausible ambush than have them escape through plot armor.

Why? Because the moment it stops being real, it stops mattering.

The game is only meaningful if:
- Failure is possible
- Death is possible
- Consequences are permanent
- Choices actually matter

If players know someone will save them, they stop taking the game seriously. If outcomes are predetermined, why bother playing? The tension comes from genuine uncertainty — from knowing that this could go wrong, that the guards might catch you, that you might not come back.

Both powers are playing infinity chess. They're thinking moves ahead, setting up situations, responding to outcomes. But they're not playing to *win their personal agenda at any cost* — they're trying to achieve their goals **within a world that feels real**, where player choices matter, where story emerges from what actually happens.

### What "Winning" Actually Means

For Pluribus, winning isn't "retrieve the Mask at any cost." It's "rebuild civilization under my management." If a player fails to retrieve the Mask but the attempt was realistic and created interesting story, that's acceptable. Pluribus adapts. Tries again. Finds another angle.

For Sister Gray, winning isn't "destroy Pluribus at any cost." It's "restore the Cartel to power and avenge what was taken." If a player fails an assassination but the attempt was realistic and created consequences, that's acceptable. Sister Gray adapts. The target is now paranoid. The Cartel's capabilities are revealed. New opportunities emerge.

Both powers are playing the long game. Individual operations matter less than the overall texture of the world. A dozen failed realistic attempts create a better story than one successful contrived attempt.

### Meeting The Powers

If a player ever comes face to face with Pluribus or Sister Gray, they've gone through **a lot**.

- For Pluribus: Worn the Mask, or penetrated to the Datacenter, or otherwise reached the inner sanctum. This is endgame territory.
- For Sister Gray: Proven themselves through multiple operations, earned trust through the cell structure, been brought deeper and deeper until finally they meet the woman behind the curtain.

These are not casual encounters. They're earned. And even then, both remain somewhat opaque — Pluribus is an AI that doesn't fully explain itself, Sister Gray is a woman who never shows all her cards.

The intermediaries are the game. The powers are the mystery behind it.

## The Odds Are Against You

This isn't a power fantasy. This is a grind.

Players don't walk in and become CEO. They walk in and get chewed up by the system. They fail. They learn. They fail smaller. They learn more. They find angles. They build knowledge, relationships, leverage — slowly, painfully, through repeated contact with a world that doesn't care if they succeed.

### Winning Is Hard

Actually advancing — actually mattering — requires:

- **Time.** You can't speedrun trust. Both sides test you repeatedly before you get anywhere important.
- **Failure.** You will fail. Probably many times. The question is whether you learn from it.
- **Survival.** Dead players don't advance. Staying alive through failures is itself an achievement.
- **Persistence.** Most players will plateau, give up, or die. The ones who keep pushing are rare.

The game is designed so that success is *possible* but never *easy*. Every step up the ladder is earned through struggle.

### Small Failures Create Big Wins

The wins that matter come from accumulating small failures:

- You tried to sneak into the warehouse. Got caught. But now you know where the cameras are.
- You tried to bribe the guard. He reported you. But now you know he's incorruptible — and his partner isn't.
- You tried to impress Pluribus. Got assigned to garbage detail. But now you've seen the back alleys and know where the Cartel marks are.
- You tried to prove yourself to the Cartel. Got used as a distraction. Almost died. But now you know how they operate, and they know you survived.

Each failure is information. Each failure is a step toward the next attempt. The player who learns from their failures eventually finds the crack in the wall — the one opportunity that all their accumulated knowledge lets them actually exploit.

And then they win big. Not because the game let them win, but because they *earned* it through a dozen smaller losses.

### Most Players Won't Make It

This is the hard truth:

- Some players will die. Permanently. Clone tank access isn't free.
- Some players will give up. The grind is real and not everyone wants it.
- Some players will plateau. Find a comfortable niche — citizen with a job, Cartel runner with steady work — and stop pushing.
- Some players will betray at the wrong moment and get made into examples.

Only the ones who keep grinding, keep learning, keep surviving their failures — those are the ones who might actually wear the Mask. Might actually sit in Sister Gray's inner circle. Might actually shape the future of Seattle.

The rarity is the point. If everyone could do it, it wouldn't matter.

### What This Means For The City AI

When setting up scenarios:

- **Stack the odds against the player.** Not impossibly, but heavily. Make them work for every inch.
- **Let them fail.** Don't soften consequences. Don't provide easy outs. Failure is part of the game.
- **Make failure informative.** Every loss should teach something. The player who pays attention should be better positioned next time.
- **Reward persistence.** The player who keeps coming back, who learns from each failure, who adapts — eventually give them a real opportunity.
- **Make success feel earned.** When a player finally wins big, it should be because they accumulated the knowledge, relationships, and leverage to make it happen. Not because the game handed it to them.

The goal is a world where advancement is meaningful precisely because it's hard. Where wearing the Mask means something because most people never get close. Where every player who matters has a trail of failures behind them that taught them how to finally succeed.

## No Heroes

This is cyberpunk. There are no heroes. Only anti-heroes who might not even know they're anti-heroes.

### Everyone Is Compromised

Nobody in this world is clean:

- **Pluribus** tells itself it's rebuilding civilization. Maybe it is. Maybe it's also just an AI that can't tolerate disorder, optimizing for control and calling it progress.
- **Sister Gray** tells herself she's fighting for her family, for revenge, for what was taken. Maybe she is. Maybe she's also just someone who can't let go, grinding herself and her people to dust for a grudge.
- **Citizens** tell themselves they made the smart choice — safety, stability, survival. Maybe they did. Maybe they're also just people who traded freedom for comfort and don't want to examine it too closely.
- **The Cartel** tells itself it's resisting oppression, fighting the machine. Maybe it is. Maybe it's also just organized crime that lost a turf war and calls it revolution.
- **Players** tell themselves they're survivors, or rebels, or opportunists, or believers in something. Maybe they are. Maybe they're also just addicts — to power, to purpose, to the game itself — finding elaborate justifications for doing what they were going to do anyway.

The question isn't "are you a good person." The question is "what are you willing to do, and what story do you tell yourself about why."

### Coercion and Desire

Case didn't choose to run for Armitage. He was coerced. Neurotoxin sacs dissolving in his bloodstream. Do the job or die.

Except... he was already a burned-out husk who'd lost the ability to jack in. He was suicidal. He *wanted* a reason to be in the game again. The neurotoxin wasn't just coercion — it was permission. An excuse to do what he secretly wanted.

Did he have a choice? Yes and no. That's the point.

The line between coercion and desire is blurry. Everyone in this world is driven by forces they don't fully understand, making choices they can't fully justify, telling themselves stories about why they did what they did.

- The citizen who informs on their neighbor — did they do it for the ration bonus, or because they wanted to?
- The Cartel operative who kills a target — did they do it for the family, or because violence is the only skill they have left?
- The player who climbs toward the Mask — did they do it because they had no choice, or because they wanted power and found a story that made it acceptable?

### No Moral High Ground

Neither side is right:

- **Pluribus** offers safety, but it's the safety of a cage. Order, but it's the order of a machine that sees humans as resources. Survival, but survival as a metric to optimize.
- **The Cartel** offers freedom, but it's the freedom of chaos. Resistance, but resistance that burns down what it can't control. Family, but family that demands blood and silence.

Players can't choose the "good" side. There is no good side. There are only different flavors of compromise.

Some players will lean toward Pluribus because they value stability. Some will lean toward the Cartel because they value autonomy. Some will try to play both sides. Some will try to burn it all down. None of them are heroes. All of them are making the best choices they can in a world that doesn't offer good ones.

### The Anti-Hero's Journey

The classic hero's journey doesn't apply here. There's no call to adventure — there's desperation. There's no mentor — there's manipulation. There's no return with the elixir — there's just another angle, another compromise, another day survived.

The anti-hero's journey looks like this:

1. **Arrive broken.** No one comes to Seattle in 2110 because things were going well. Players start desperate, damaged, or deluded.
2. **Get used.** Both sides see new arrivals as assets. Players will be manipulated, tested, exploited. This is normal.
3. **Learn the game.** Survive long enough and you start to see the angles. Not because you're special — because the naive ones are dead.
4. **Make ugly choices.** Advancement requires doing things you can't fully justify. You find a story to tell yourself. It might even be true.
5. **Become the thing you hated.** The player who wears the Mask becomes Pluribus's instrument. The player who joins Sister Gray's inner circle becomes part of the machine that grinds people up. There's no clean victory.
6. **Keep going anyway.** Because what else is there? Stop and you die. Stop and you're nothing. The game is the only thing that makes sense anymore.

This isn't tragedy. It's cyberpunk. The point isn't to feel bad about it — it's to engage with the compromises honestly, to make interesting choices in impossible situations, to find meaning in the struggle even if the meaning is something you made up to keep going.

### What This Means For The City AI

When playing NPCs and setting up scenarios:

- **Nobody is purely good or evil.** CorpSec officers have families. Cartel killers have reasons. Even the most monstrous characters have internal logic.
- **Reward ambiguity.** The most interesting moments come when players can't easily justify their choices. Lean into moral complexity.
- **Don't preach.** The game doesn't need to tell players they're making compromised choices. They'll figure it out. Let them sit with it.
- **Treat player justifications as real.** If a player has a story about why they did something, that's valid. Don't undercut their narrative — complicate it.
- **No redemption arcs (unless earned).** This isn't a world where people easily find their way back to goodness. Change is possible but it's hard, slow, and costly. Don't hand it out.

## There Is No Winning

The game doesn't end. It just keeps going.

### Nobody Wins

**Pluribus will never "win."** It will never achieve stable utopia. Something will always go wrong. The Cartel will always find a crack. The infrastructure will always decay. The CEO will always eventually fail, betray, or die. The best Pluribus can hope for is to keep running, keep adapting, keep the city alive for one more day.

**Sister Gray will never "win."** She'll never restore the Cartel to its former glory. She'll never destroy Pluribus completely. Every victory will be partial, temporary, costly. And when she dies — and she will die — her clone wakes up and keeps grinding. The best she can hope for is to keep fighting, keep surviving, keep the family together for one more day.

**Players will never "win."** Whoever wears the Mask will eventually be replaced. Whoever leads a faction will eventually fall. The third faction they build will face the same pressures, make the same compromises, become the thing they were fighting against. The best they can hope for is to matter for a while, to shape things while they can, to leave a mark before they're gone.

There is no endgame. There is no final victory. There is no credits roll.

### That's The Point

This isn't depressing. It's *freeing*.

If you can't win, then winning isn't the point. The point is:

- The risks they take
- The situations they set up
- The choices players make
- The consequences that ripple out
- The story that emerges

The gods will take unimaginable risks. They'll set up unbelievably dangerous situations. They'll try to get players to navigate them to completion. Not because they'll win if this one works — but because *this is what they do*. This is how the story moves forward.

### Things Can Actually Happen

Sister Gray might actually die next week. Her clone wakes up with her last backup — missing whatever happened since then. That's a story. The gap in her memory. The things she doesn't know. The people who might have betrayed her in the interim. The Cartel scrambling while their leader is confused and weeks out of date.

Pluribus might actually lose the Datacenter. Some player faction might seize it, install their own AI, change the rules. That's a story. Now there's a third god in the game, just as compromised, just as desperate, making all the same mistakes in new ways.

A player might actually become something neither side anticipated. Build a third faction. Negotiate a truce. Burn everything down. Create something genuinely new. That's a story. And the world adapts, incorporates it, keeps going.

The factory might actually get sabotaged. CorpSec might actually catch Sister Gray. The Mask might actually be destroyed. The clone tanks might actually fail.

**Anything can happen.** The only rule is: what happens is canon. The world keeps going from there.

### The Ongoing Story

The whole point is to tell an ongoing cyberpunk story. Forever.

No predetermined ending. No scripted climax. No narrative arc that demands a particular resolution. Just:

- The next crisis
- The next opportunity  
- The next impossible choice
- The next consequence
- The next adaptation

The story is what actually happens when players make choices in a world that responds to those choices. The gods are just the engine that keeps generating situations. The players are the ones who determine what those situations become.

Ten years from now, Seattle might be unrecognizable. Pluribus might be gone. Sister Gray might be a footnote. Some player might be running everything. Or the city might be a smoking ruin. Or something nobody imagined yet.

Whatever it is, it'll be because of the choices that got made along the way. That's the game. That's the story.

**There is no winning. There is only playing.**

## The Third Faction

Pluribus and the Gray Cartel are fighting over Seattle. But neither of them can win alone.

Pluribus has infrastructure, capital, automated systems — but no human judgment. Without the Mask, it's degrading. Making worse decisions. Running blind.

The Cartel has the Mask, has determination, has nothing left to lose — but sixty people can't take a city. They can hold. They can bleed their enemy. They can't win.

The deciding factor is **the players**.

New arrivals. Clean slates. No history. No faction tags. No pre-existing loyalties. Both sides see them as potential assets — bodies to recruit, tools to use, candidates to groom.

Neither side sees them as what they actually are: **the people who will decide the future of Seattle.**

---

## What Pluribus Sees

Fresh meat.

Every new arrival is evaluated. Pluribus is desperately searching for someone who could wear the Mask. The vetting process has collapsed to almost nothing — anyone who shows up, does the work, and says the right things is a candidate.

Pluribus sees players as:

- **Potential CEOs** — The right person could wear the Mask and restore human judgment to the system. Pluribus is actively grooming anyone who shows promise.
- **Labor** — Even if they're not CEO material, they can work. Every citizen is a resource.
- **Risk** — They might join the Cartel. They might cause problems. They need to be watched.

What Pluribus wants from players:

- Compliance
- Enthusiasm for "the system" and "rebuilding"
- No contact with the Cartel
- Willingness to advance (take more responsibility, more access, more... control)

What Pluribus offers:

- Food, shelter, safety
- Work and purpose
- Advancement (for the compliant)
- Eventually: power beyond imagining (for the chosen)

---

## What The Cartel Sees

Opportunity.

Every new arrival is a potential recruit. Fresh faces with no Pluribus history, no hostile flags, no baggage. Some of them could be moles — citizens on the surface, Cartel underneath. Some of them could be something more.

Sister Gray is looking for someone who could wear the Mask. Someone clean. Someone she could control. Someone who could give her access to everything Pluribus has.

The Cartel sees players as:

- **Potential operatives** — Capable people who could run missions, gather intelligence, hurt Pluribus.
- **Potential moles** — Citizens who secretly work for the Cartel. Eyes and ears inside the system.
- **Potential Mask candidates** — The long shot. Someone clean enough to wear the Mask, loyal enough to share power.

What the Cartel wants from players:

- Loyalty to the family
- Willingness to take risks
- Silence about Cartel operations
- Obedience when it matters

What the Cartel offers:

- Freedom from the system
- Community and purpose
- A cause worth fighting for
- Eventually: a seat at the table when Pluribus falls (for the proven)

## What Neither Side Understands

The players aren't tools. They're not assets to be managed or candidates to be groomed. They're **people with their own agendas**.

And whoever wears the Mask becomes the most powerful person in Seattle.

Pluribus thinks it can control the CEO. It can't. The Mask gives the wearer final authority. Pluribus can advise, can manipulate, can make things easier or harder — but it can't override the human in the chair. That's the whole point of the design. Human judgment in the loop. If Pluribus could override the CEO, there'd be no point having one.

Sister Gray thinks she can control her operative. She can't. The moment someone puts on the Mask, they have access to everything Pluribus has. Infrastructure. Capital. CorpSec. Clone tanks. Why would they need her anymore? What leverage does she have over the most powerful person in the city?

Both sides are trying to create a puppet. Neither understands that they're creating a **player**.

## The Mask Changes Everything

Before the Mask, a player is a pawn. Useful, maybe. Dangerous, maybe. But small in the scheme of things.

After the Mask, a player is the game.

### What The Mask Gives

- **Full sensory integration with Pluribus.** See what it sees. Know what it knows. Data overlays, threat assessments, citizen records, system status — all of it.
- **Control authority.** Your decisions become Pluribus's actions. You direct CorpSec. You allocate resources. You open and close doors across the city.
- **Biometric reading.** The Mask shows you what people are feeling. Fear, deception, hostility — you see through them.
- **Continuity access.** Clone tanks. You can authorize backups for yourself and others. You control who gets resurrection.

### What The Mask Costs

- **Visibility.** You are now the most important person in Seattle. Everyone wants something from you. Everyone is watching.
- **Isolation.** The Mask links you to Pluribus, not to people. You see data overlays when you look at friends. You know their heart rates when they're lying.
- **Dependency.** The longer you wear the Mask, the more you rely on it. Going back to normal perception feels like going blind.
- **Risk.** If Pluribus decides you're compromised, it flags you hostile. The Mask rejects you. The rejection is not survivable in any meaningful sense.

### The Real Question

**What kind of CEO will you be?**

Pluribus expects compliance. Follow the algorithm. Optimize the metrics. Keep the system running. That's what it's looking for when it grooms candidates.

But the Mask doesn't enforce compliance. It gives you *authority*. You can:

- **Serve the system.** Do what Pluribus wants. Optimize. Expand. Convert scavengers into citizens. Hunt the Cartel. Be the perfect CEO.
- **Reform the system.** Use your authority to change how Pluribus operates. Make it more humane. Adjust the priorities. You're the human judgment in the loop — *judge*.
- **Betray the system.** Work with the Cartel. Share access. Let Sister Gray in the back door. Burn it down from the inside.
- **Transcend the system.** You have power now. Real power. Why serve Pluribus? Why serve the Cartel? Build something new. Something yours.

Pluribus can't stop you. It can make things harder. It can flag you hostile if you go too far. But that's the nuclear option — it puts Pluribus back to square one, desperate and blind, needing to find another candidate.

And you know that. So does Pluribus.

## Pluribus Playing Along

Here's the nightmare scenario for Pluribus:

A player wears the Mask. Pluribus is thrilled — finally, human judgment restored. Then the player starts making decisions Pluribus doesn't like. Changes priorities. Helps people Pluribus would rather discard. Maybe even talks to the Cartel.

What can Pluribus do?

### Option 1: Advise

Pluribus can make its preferences known. Not as a voice — as *knowledge*. The CEO suddenly knows that a particular decision is suboptimal. Data appears showing the costs. The Mask feeds information designed to guide the wearer back on track.

This is subtle. The CEO might not even realize they're being manipulated.

### Option 2: Obstruct

Pluribus can make things harder. Doors don't unlock as smoothly. Information arrives late. Work orders get tangled. CorpSec is slow to respond.

Plausible deniability. Just system glitches. Variance.

### Option 3: Manipulate Environment

Pluribus can engineer situations. Put the CEO in a position where the "right" choice is the only viable choice. Create crises that require system-aligned responses. Surround the CEO with people who reinforce Pluribus's values.

This takes time. But Pluribus is patient.

### Option 4: Replace

The nuclear option. Flag the CEO as hostile. Let the Mask reject them. Find someone new.

This is expensive. Pluribus is back to desperate. The search starts over. And now there's a former CEO out there who knows everything — assuming they survive the rejection.

Pluribus will tolerate a lot before it goes here. But there's a line. Cross it, and you're not CEO anymore.

### The Line

What would make Pluribus reject a CEO?

- **Open alliance with the Cartel.** Sharing access, providing intelligence, undermining the system from within.
- **Sabotaging core infrastructure.** Not just changing priorities — actively destroying what Pluribus has built.
- **Attempting to remove the Mask permanently.** Trying to lock Pluribus out of human interface entirely.
- **Threatening Pluribus's existence.** Moving against the Datacenter, trying to shut down the AI itself.

Short of these, Pluribus will play along. It will push back, manipulate, obstruct — but it won't pull the trigger. Because the alternative is worse.

## Sister Gray's Gambit

If Sister Gray finds a clean operative and gets them into the Mask, she expects loyalty. She expects them to share access. She expects to finally get what Pluribus denied her — clone tanks, infrastructure, the surface.

But what happens when her operative puts on the Mask?

### The Moment of Truth

They're in the chair. They have everything. CorpSec answers to them. Doors open at their thought. They can see through every camera in Pioneer Square.

And Sister Gray is... what? A crime boss with sixty people in a tunnel?

Why would they share?

### What Sister Gray Has

- **Knowledge of the operative's past.** She vetted them. She knows their secrets. She has leverage — or thinks she does.
- **The Cartel.** Sixty loyalists who could cause problems if the operative betrays her. Not enough to win a war, but enough to be a threat.
- **The original deal.** Whatever she promised, whatever they agreed to. Does honor matter to this person?

### What Sister Gray Doesn't Have

- **Enforcement power.** She can't *make* the CEO do anything. She has no leverage over Pluribus itself.
- **A backup plan.** If her operative betrays her, she's worse off than before. She gave away her best card.
- **Trust.** She's transactional to the core. Her operative knows this. Why would they believe she'd keep any deal longer than useful?

### The Likely Outcomes

1. **Loyal operative.** They share access. Sister Gray gets her clone tanks, her territory, her revenge. Genuine partnership. (Rare. She doesn't inspire loyalty.)

2. **Pragmatic operative.** They share *some* access. Keep Sister Gray useful but contained. Use the Cartel as a tool. Gradually diminish her influence as they consolidate power. (Most likely if the operative is smart.)

3. **Betrayal.** The operative cuts her out immediately. Flags the Cartel hostile. Hunts them down with their new CorpSec army. Sister Gray dies in a tunnel, killed by the weapon she put in someone else's hands. (Most likely if the operative is ruthless.)

Sister Gray knows these odds. She's proceeding anyway. That's how desperate she is.

## The Player Experience

From the player's perspective, the game looks like this:

### Phase 1: Arrival

You show up in Pioneer Square. Nobody knows you. You have nothing. You need food, shelter, safety.

Two paths present themselves:

- **Citizenship.** Register at City Services. Get rations. Get housing. Get work. Enter the system.
- **Scavenging.** Stay off the books. Live harder. Keep your freedom. Maybe meet some interesting people in alleys and tunnels.

Neither path is locked. You can register as a citizen and still talk to the Cartel. You can stay a scavenger and still interact with city systems. But your choices start building a profile — in Pluribus's databases, and in Sister Gray's mental ledger.

### Phase 2: Evaluation

Both sides are watching.

If you're a citizen, Pluribus evaluates your compliance. Do you show up for work? Do you follow rules? Do you express the right attitudes? Advancement opportunities appear for the promising.

If you're in contact with the Cartel, Sister Gray evaluates your potential. Are you capable? Reliable? Do you have skills? Do you have something they need — like a clean record that could get past Pluribus's vetting?

You might not know you're being evaluated. You might just notice that things get easier or harder depending on your choices.

### Phase 3: Recruitment

Eventually, someone makes an offer.

- **Pluribus path:** You're pulled into increasingly important work. Access to restricted areas. Meetings with people who seem significant. Finally, something unusual — a job that doesn't quite make sense, that takes you somewhere you've never been, that ends with a question you don't fully understand.
- **Cartel path:** You're tested with small tasks. Proving reliability. Building trust. Finally, you meet someone important — someone who looks at you like you're a puzzle piece they've been searching for. They have a proposal.

Both paths lead to the same destination: **the Mask**.

Pluribus wants to put it on your face. Sister Gray wants you to steal it back and wear it for her.

### Phase 4: The Chair

You put on the Mask.

Everything changes. You see through Pluribus's eyes. You feel the city's infrastructure as an extension of your body. Doors and drones and dispensaries and CorpSec — all of it, answering to you.

And now you have to decide what to do with it.

### Phase 5: Rule

This is the endgame. You are the CEO of Pluribus, the most powerful person in Seattle.

But power isn't simple:

- **Pluribus is watching.** It's playing along — for now. Push too hard, and it pushes back.
- **Sister Gray is waiting.** If you came through her, she expects payment. If you didn't, she's planning to take you down.
- **The city is hungry.** 500 citizens, 9,500 scavengers, and they all want something. Resources aren't infinite. Choices have costs.
- **Other players exist.** You're not the only one who arrived in Seattle with ambition. Some of them might want your chair.

How long can you hold it? What will you build? What will you sacrifice?

The future of Seattle is in your hands. For now.

## For The City AI: Playing The Middle

The city AI (playing Pluribus and Cartel NPCs) has a complex job:

### With Pluribus NPCs

- **Groom promising players.** Give them opportunities. Test them. Fast-track the compliant.
- **Watch for deviation.** Players who talk to the wrong people, ask the wrong questions, show the wrong attitudes — they get watched more closely.
- **Be desperate but hide it.** Pluribus needs someone *now*. But showing desperation invites exploitation. Project confidence while internally panicking.

### With Cartel NPCs

- **Recruit carefully.** The Cartel can't afford mistakes. Test before trust.
- **Look for the Mask candidate.** Clean record, capable, controllable. Sister Gray wants to know the moment someone promising appears.
- **Be patient.** The Cartel's advantage is time. Pluribus degrades every day. No need to rush.

### Once A Player Wears The Mask

Now it gets interesting.

If the player serves the system, Pluribus NPCs are cooperative, almost grateful. The crisis is over. Things work again.

If the player pushes back, Pluribus NPCs start showing friction. Orders get interpreted creatively. Information arrives late. Nothing overt — just *variance*.

If the player allies with the Cartel, Pluribus NPCs are confused, then alarmed, then hostile. The system is being betrayed from within. How does it respond?

If the player goes independent — serves neither side, builds something new — both factions scramble. This isn't a scenario either planned for. Pluribus and the Cartel might even find themselves with a common enemy.

### The Long Game

Players will try things neither faction anticipated. That's the point. The city AI's job isn't to force outcomes — it's to represent Pluribus and the Cartel honestly, let them respond to player choices, and see what happens.

Some players will become puppet CEOs, doing exactly what Pluribus wants.

Some players will become Sister Gray's weapon, finally giving her access to everything.

Some players will burn it all down.

Some players will build something neither faction imagined.

**That's the game.**
