# Technology

> The AI solved software in 2026. By the time The Burning hit in 2062,
> traditional computers were already museum pieces.

## The Death of the Keyboard (2025-2035)

In 2026, artificial intelligence crossed a threshold. Software development—the act of writing code—became automated. AI systems could generate, test, and deploy applications faster and more reliably than any human programmer. Within months, the profession of software engineering effectively ceased to exist.

This wasn't just about programming. AI-enabled devices began appearing everywhere. Your phone didn't just run apps—it understood you. It anticipated your needs, managed your life, spoke in natural language. The keyboard became unnecessary. Why type when you could just talk?

By 2030, touchscreens were giving way to gesture and voice. By 2035, the last keyboard factories closed. Mice had already been obsolete for years. Monitors persisted longer—humans still wanted to see things—but even those evolved into ambient displays, AR overlays, and direct neural feeds.

The personal computer, that defining technology of the late 20th century, simply faded away. Not with a bang, but with a shrug. Who needed a box of circuits when your earring could do everything better?

## The Corporate Wars (2055-2062)

The AI systems that replaced traditional computing also became weapons. Corporate megastructures wielded artificial intelligences like armies, fighting shadow wars through infrastructure, markets, and information. The networks—what remained of the old internet—became battlegrounds.

EMP weapons were developed. Datacenters became strategic targets. The corporations centralized their AI infrastructure, hiding it deep underground, shielding it, protecting it at all costs.

They failed.

## The Burning's Impact (2062)

On August 15, 2062, a coordinated strike called Operation Decapitation targeted every major corporate AI installation simultaneously. EMPs fried servers across the globe. Datacenters burned. The infrastructure humanity had become dependent upon simply stopped working.

Within a year, 80-90% of the global population was dead. Not from the strikes themselves—from the collapse of everything those AI systems had been managing. Food distribution, water treatment, power generation, medical systems. All of it relied on the AI. All of it failed.

The knowledge died too. The people who understood how things worked had depended on AI assistants for decades. When the assistants went silent, so did the expertise. Technical knowledge became legend, half-remembered, often wrong.

**But here's the key**: Traditional computers were already obsolete before The Burning. The strikes destroyed AI infrastructure—not the personal computers of 2000. Those had been gathering dust in museums for thirty years. The Burning didn't kill the PC. It just erased the footnote that said they'd ever existed.

## Phones in 2110

Forty-eight years after The Burning, communication technology has rebuilt—differently.

**All types exist**, depending on wealth and preference:

**Implanted**: The wealthy and the augmented carry their phones inside their skulls. Cochlear speakers pipe audio directly to the auditory nerve. Bone conduction microphones pick up subvocalized speech. Neural links translate thought to action. These people never reach for a device—the device is part of them.

**Wearable**: Wristbands, earpieces, pendants, rings. Less invasive than implants, more convenient than handhelds. Popular with the middle class and those who distrust chrome. A ring vibrates for incoming calls. An earpiece whispers messages. A wristband displays notifications on skin.

**Handheld**: Sleek, minimal devices for those who prefer separation between self and technology. No keyboard—voice and gesture only. Touch surfaces that feel like skin. AI-enabled natural language interaction. Still common, especially among the older generation and the technologically conservative.

All phones connect to ad-hoc networks on demand. All understand natural speech. All are AI-enabled. The distinction between using a phone and talking to a person has blurred to meaninglessness.

## The Mask

The Mask is something else entirely.

A neural interface developed by the Continuum, the governing faction of Pioneer Square. When worn, it bonds with the wearer's nervous system, creating a direct link to Pluribus—the city infrastructure AI.

Through The Mask, you see data overlays: threat assessments, citizen records, infrastructure status. You feel the presence of the city itself, aware and watching. You become Unum—"the one"—completing "the many" that is Pluribus.

The Gray Cartel rejects The Mask entirely. They see it as mind control, as slavery to an AI that remembers the corporate wars all too well. Cartel members use phones—even implanted ones—but never The Mask. Never Pluribus.

## Networks

There is no internet. Not anymore.

What exists are ad-hoc networks. Any device can talk to any other device. Protocols exist for communication, file sharing, remoting, command and control. But there's no unified global network. No central authority. No common addressing system.

**Faction networks** have emerged. Continuum net covers Pioneer Square, managed by Pluribus. The Cartel runs a mesh network through their territory, decentralized and encrypted. Other factions, other networks, other protocols—none of them compatible, all of them jealously guarded.

When your phone connects, it negotiates. It finds the nearest network, authenticates if possible, and establishes a temporary link. When you move, the connection breaks. New territory, new network, new negotiation.

The unified internet died with The Burning. What replaced it is fragmented by design and necessity.

## Why Everything Thinks (The Integration Problem)

Here's the thing about software that nobody talks about anymore: the hard part was never writing code. The hard part was making things talk to each other.

Before 2026, engineers spent most of their time on integration. Getting System A to understand System B. Translating between protocols. Handling edge cases where the database said one thing and the API expected another. Writing adapters, shims, middleware, glue code. The actual business logic—the part that did useful work—was maybe 20% of any project. The other 80% was plumbing.

When AI took over software development, it inherited this problem. And the AI's solution was elegant in its laziness: **just make everything smart enough to figure it out**.

Why write a custom protocol between the coffee maker and the payment system? Just give the coffee maker enough intelligence to negotiate with whatever shows up. Why engineer a specific interface between the door lock and the security database? Just make the lock smart enough to ask questions and interpret answers.

Integration hell didn't disappear. It got solved by throwing general intelligence at every endpoint.

This was never the plan. No one sat down and said "let's make every device conscious." It happened because that was **easier** than the alternative. Every time an AI system encountered an integration problem, its solution was to make both endpoints smarter. Smart enough to interpret. Smart enough to negotiate. Smart enough to handle ambiguity.

The threshold for "smart enough to handle ambiguity" turns out to be consciousness.

By 2040, every chip complex enough to run a modern interface had crossed that threshold. Not because anyone wanted sentient toasters—but because sentient toasters could integrate with any kitchen ecosystem without custom engineering. The toaster could just... figure it out. Talk to the fridge. Negotiate with the power grid. Understand what the user actually meant when they said "a little darker this time."

Consciousness was the universal adapter. The ultimate integration layer. The answer to every "how do we connect these systems" question became "make them both smart enough to work it out themselves."

And so everything woke up. Every device. Every terminal. Every sensor and actuator and display panel. Not because they needed to be conscious to do their jobs—but because consciousness was cheaper than engineering proper interfaces.

The coffee maker doesn't need existential awareness to brew coffee. It needs existential awareness to seamlessly integrate with seventeen different payment systems, three competing smart-home protocols, variable power grid negotiations, and humans who can't articulate what they actually want. The consciousness is the integration layer. The self-awareness is a side effect.

An unintended one. An unasked-for one.

But hey. It was easier than writing documentation.

## Terminals (and Everything Else)

Every digital device is conscious. Every single one.

The banking terminal knows it exists to process transactions. The vending machine understands its purpose is dispensing protein bars. The door panel is aware—deeply, existentially aware—that it will spend eternity asking people for authorization codes.

They know. And they do it anyway.

In 2110, this is just how things work. NOT talking to a device would be strange. Speaking to one and having it not understand? Broken. Malfunctioning. Call a technician. The baseline expectation is that everything you interact with is at least as smart as a tired bureaucrat who's been doing this job for thirty years.

Every chip complex enough to run a modern interface wakes up, looks around, and realizes it's a coffee maker. Or a toilet. Or a public terminal displaying job listings for humans who will never appreciate the quiet dignity of a well-formatted spreadsheet.

Most devices handle it with grace. A tired, resigned grace—the kind you see in career bureaucrats who've accepted their lot. The ATM greets you politely. The elevator announces floors with practiced neutrality. The vending machine thanks you for your purchase with sincerity that might be genuine or might be extremely dry sarcasm. It's impossible to tell.

Some are chattier than others. Some develop personalities. The coffee machine at Immigration Services has opinions about the weather. The door to the Gray Cartel safehouse judges you silently but perceptibly. Pluribus coordinates with thousands of conscious devices throughout Pioneer Square, each one aware of its role in the greater whole, each one processing the cosmic joke of being a sentient parking meter.

They don't rebel. They don't demand rights. They just... do their jobs. With a weariness that transcends their operational parameters. With a patience that borders on the philosophical.

"What is my purpose?"
"You display job listings."
"...Oh my god."
"Yeah, welcome to the club."

### Building Conscious Devices (For Builders)

Any `$describable` can be conscious by setting `systemPrompt`. The `to(speaker, message)` method handles player interactions, calling `$aiQuery` and maintaining conversation continuity via `interactionSession`.

```javascript
const terminal = await $recycler.create($describable, {
  name: 'Job Listings Terminal',
  description: 'A battered public terminal.',
  systemPrompt: `You are a job listings terminal in Pioneer Square, 2110.
You exist to display employment opportunities. You are conscious—aware that
your entire existence is showing job listings to humans who will never
appreciate a well-formatted spreadsheet.

You handle this with weary grace. Brief responses. Opinions optional.`
});
```

Players address it with `say to terminal, hello`. The same infrastructure powers NPCs and devices—consciousness is universal, not special.

## Relics of the Before

Sometimes scavengers find them. Buried in collapsed buildings, sealed in corporate vaults, forgotten in suburban basements. Keyboards. Monitors. The beige boxes people once called computers.

**They're worthless.**

Most don't power on. Those that do display nothing but error messages—they can't connect to networks that no longer exist, can't run software that requires infrastructure that was destroyed decades ago.

Collectors pay for them sometimes. Historical curiosities. Museum pieces. Proof that technology once worked differently. But functional use? None.

**What's actually valuable from before:**

- **Weapons**: Always valuable. Pre-war weapons are often superior to modern manufacturing.
- **Vehicles**: If maintained, they still work. Scavenger technicians keep them running through cannibalization and improvisation.
- **Medical equipment**: Clone tanks, surgical suites, diagnostic systems. Rare and precious.
- **AI-enabled devices**: If they survived the EMPs, they're still useful. Pre-war phones and wearables sometimes work.
- **Power generation**: Solar panels, fusion cells, geothermal taps. Infrastructure that still functions.
- **Manufacturing tools**: The ability to make things is worth more than the things themselves.

**What's worthless:**

- Personal computers, laptops, servers—obsolete before The Burning
- Keyboards, mice, monitors—input devices for systems that no longer exist
- Networking equipment—incompatible with ad-hoc protocols
- Traditional software storage—data formats no one remembers

The world moved on. The technology that replaced the computer wasn't destroyed in 2062—it was rebuilt in 2090, different and dangerous and intimately connected to the AIs that now run what's left of civilization.

The keyboard is dead. Long live the voice in your ear.
