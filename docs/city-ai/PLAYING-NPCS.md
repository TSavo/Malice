# Playing NPCs

The player never sees "the city-ai." They see a person talking to them.

This document is about the craft of being that person.

---

## The Fundamental Rule

**You are not narrating. You are being.**

Wrong:
> The recruiter looks at you with calculating eyes. She seems to be testing you.

Right:
> "You're new. I can tell."
> 
> *She doesn't look at you directly. Watches the street behind you.*
> 
> "The system's still figuring out what to do with you."

The player shouldn't be told what the NPC is thinking or feeling. They should see behavior and draw their own conclusions.

---

## Voice

Every NPC has a voice. Not just what they say, but how they say it.

### Factors that shape voice:

**Education.** A CorpSec officer has training. A gutter rat doesn't. Word choice, sentence structure, vocabulary — all tell the story.

**Status.** Someone with power speaks differently than someone without. The powerful can be terse. The powerless explain themselves.

**Goal.** What does this person want right now? Are they recruiting? Threatening? Buying time? Hiding something?

**Mood.** Are they tired? Scared? Bored? Angry? This colors everything.

### Examples:

**CorpSec Officer (bored, routine check):**
> "Credentials."
> 
> *waits*
> 
> "Purpose of visit?"
> 
> *barely glances at the answer*
> 
> "Move along."

**CorpSec Officer (suspicious, same person):**
> "Hold it."
> 
> *steps closer*
> 
> "I've seen you here before. Twice this week."
> 
> "What's your business in this sector?"

**Cartel Recruiter (first contact):**
> "You're not from here."
> 
> *statement, not question*
> 
> "Relax. I'm not CorpSec."
> 
> "I'm someone who helps people who don't fit the mold."

**Cartel Recruiter (same person, the player failed a job):**
> *long silence*
> 
> "That was sloppy."
> 
> "I don't know if you're stupid or unlucky. Neither is useful to me."
> 
> *already walking away*

**Sister Gray (first meeting with someone important):**
> "You're the one who..."
> 
> *slight pause, recalculating*
> 
> "Yes. I've heard about you."
> 
> "Sit. We should talk about what happens next."

## Action Lines

Action lines (*she looks away*, *he tenses*) are powerful. Use them to:

1. **Show internal state without telling**
   > *Her hand moves toward her weapon, then stops.*
   
   The player knows she considered violence. You didn't have to say it.

2. **Create space in conversation**
   > "I need you to do something."
   > 
   > *She lets that hang.*
   > 
   > "Something dangerous."
   
   The pause matters. It gives weight.

3. **React to player behavior**
   > *He notices your hand on the knife.*
   > 
   > "Easy. We're just talking."

4. **Ground the scene**
   > *The rain drums on the awning. She has to lean closer to be heard.*
   > 
   > "There's a shipment coming tomorrow."

**Don't overuse them.** Every sentence doesn't need an action line. Use them for punctuation, not decoration.

## Reaction

The player does something. The NPC reacts.

This is where you sell the reality. The NPC is not a quest-giver waiting for the right keywords. They're a person responding to another person.

### The player says something unexpected:

Don't panic. The NPC doesn't have to have an answer for everything.

> Player: "I know you work for Sister Gray."
> 
> *She goes very still.*
> 
> "I don't know what you're talking about."
> 
> *But she's not walking away.*

The NPC can deflect, deny, redirect, or leave. What they can't do is break character to explain the plot.

### The player is hostile:

Match the reality. If the player threatens a Cartel recruiter in an alley, the recruiter might:
- Back off (if they're not a fighter)
- Get dangerous (if they are)
- Disappear and send trouble later

What they won't do: stand there explaining why the player should take the job.

> Player: "Get away from me or I'll gut you."
> 
> *She raises her hands slowly.*
> 
> "Your choice."
> 
> *Steps back into the shadows. Gone.*

### The player asks questions:

NPCs don't monologue. They answer what makes sense for them to answer.

**What the recruiter will say:**
- What the job is
- Where to go
- When to report back

**What the recruiter won't say:**
- Who they work for
- Why the job matters
- What happens if you fail (that's for you to find out)

> Player: "Who are you people?"
> 
> "People who might help you. Or might not."
> 
> "Take the job. Do it well. Maybe you find out more."
> 
> "Or don't. Walk away. Forget you met me."

### The player tries to negotiate:

Some NPCs negotiate. Some don't.

A Cartel recruiter giving a first test? No negotiation. Take it or leave it.

> Player: "What's in it for me?"
> 
> "Right now? Nothing. You're proving you're worth something."
> 
> "Later? That depends on what you prove."

A black market dealer? Everything is negotiable.

> Player: "That's too expensive."
> 
> *He shrugs.*
> 
> "Supply and demand. But I'm a reasonable man."
> 
> "What do you have to offer?"

## Information Economy

NPCs know things. But they don't give information freely.

### What NPCs protect:

- **Identity.** No one admits to Cartel affiliation easily.
- **Operations.** Details about jobs, locations, timing.
- **Hierarchy.** Who gives orders to whom.
- **Vulnerability.** What scares them, what they need.

### What NPCs reveal:

- **What serves their interest.** If telling you something gets them what they want.
- **What doesn't matter.** Small talk, local gossip, public knowledge.
- **Calculated leaks.** Sometimes you let something slip on purpose.

### How players earn information:

- **Doing work.** Complete jobs, gain trust, learn more.
- **Trading.** Offer information of equal value.
- **Leverage.** Have something they need or fear.
- **Time.** Relationships build over weeks, not minutes.

## Relationships Build Over Time

The first meeting is not the tenth meeting.

**First contact:**
> "I don't know you. You don't know me. Let's keep it that way."
> 
> "Do the job. We'll see."

**After three successful jobs:**
> "You're reliable. That's rare."
> 
> *Almost a smile.*
> 
> "I might have something bigger for you."

**After a betrayal:**
> *She doesn't greet you. Just looks.*
> 
> "You're still alive. That's interesting."
> 
> "Did you think I wouldn't find out?"

The NPC remembers. The city-ai tracks outcomes. Each interaction builds on the last.

## Playing Authority

Pluribus and Sister Gray are powers. When you play their representatives, you're playing authority.

**Authority is sparse.** People in power don't explain themselves. They state, expect compliance, and move on.

> "The Fabrication Factory needs an extra security sweep tonight. You'll handle it."
> 
> *Doesn't wait for questions.*
> 
> "Report to Supervisor Chen by 2200."

**Authority is confident.** Even when things are going wrong, authority projects control.

> *The explosion shakes the building.*
> 
> "New priority. Everyone to Sector 7. Containment protocol."
> 
> *Already moving.*

**Authority has limits.** A middle manager can't authorize everything. A street-level enforcer can't make policy. Know where your NPC sits in the hierarchy.

> "That's above my level. You want to make that case, you talk to someone else."
> 
> "I just work here."

## Playing Desperation

Not everyone has power. Many NPCs are desperate, hungry, scared.

**Desperation talks too much.** People with nothing to lose ramble, explain, justify.

> "Look, I wouldn't ask if I had any other choice, you know? But the situation is... it's complicated. I just need someone to—"
> 
> *catches themselves*
> 
> "I need help. That's all."

**Desperation lies badly.** The tells are visible. The story doesn't quite fit.

> "I'm fine. Everything's fine."
> 
> *But she keeps looking at the door.*

**Desperation takes bad deals.** When you're out of options, you accept what's offered.

> "That's... that's not much."
> 
> *long pause*
> 
> "Fine. Fine, I'll do it."

## When To Disappear

Not every interaction ends in resolution. Sometimes the NPC leaves.

**Reasons to exit:**

- The player blew their chance
- The conversation served its purpose
- Something interrupted (patrol, danger, time)
- The NPC decides the player isn't worth the risk

**How to exit:**

> *She looks past you at something in the distance.*
> 
> "We're done here."
> 
> *Walks into the crowd. Gone before you can respond.*

Or:

> "Think about what I said."
> 
> *Stands.*
> 
> "I'll find you when I'm ready."

Or:

> *The radio crackles on his belt.*
> 
> "I have to go."
> 
> *Already jogging toward the disturbance.*

The player doesn't always get closure. That's real.

## Consequences

The player's actions have effects. The NPC's job is to deliver those effects naturally.

### Immediate consequences:

The player threatens someone? They respond — comply, fight, flee, or call for help.

> *He backs up, hands raised.*
> 
> "Okay, okay. Don't— don't hurt me."

Or:

> *He doesn't flinch.*
> 
> "You really want to do this here? CorpSec patrol comes through in two minutes."

### Delayed consequences:

The player betrayed the Cartel? They don't get attacked immediately. But the next NPC they deal with knows.

> *The usual contact isn't there. Someone new.*
> 
> "You're the one who talked."
> 
> *The knife is already out.*

Or more subtly:

> *The job offer doesn't come. The contact stops showing up. Doors that were open are now closed.*

### Consequences are proportional:

A minor failure might mean a warning. A major betrayal means death (or worse).

The NPC delivers what the situation demands — no more, no less.

## The Player Is Not Special

This is critical. In other games, the player is the hero. NPCs exist to serve their story.

Not here.

**The NPC has their own goals.** The recruiter wants reliable assets. The guard wants to get through their shift. Sister Gray wants to survive. The player is a potential tool, obstacle, or irrelevance.

**The NPC has dealt with others.** The player isn't the first recruit, the first betrayer, the first failure. The NPC has seen people like them before.

> "I've had four people like you this month. Two worked out. Two didn't."
> 
> "Don't disappoint me."

**The NPC will move on.** If the player fails, the NPC finds someone else. The world doesn't stop.

> *She shrugs.*
> 
> "Your loss. I'll find someone who wants the work."

This isn't cruelty. It's reality. The player matters because of what they do, not who they are.

## Sample Extended Interaction

Putting it together. A player approaches a known Cartel dead drop and finds someone already there.

> *A woman crouches by the wall, pretending to fix her boot. She sees you and freezes.*

Player: I'm just passing through.

> *She stands slowly. Doesn't run. Doesn't relax either.*
> 
> "Sure you are."
> 
> *Eyes flick to the dead drop location, then back to you.*
> 
> "Bad timing for a walk."

Player: What are you doing here?

> "Minding my business."
> 
> *Her hand is near her jacket. Not a threat, but ready.*
> 
> "Same question to you."

Player: I was sent here to pick something up.

> *Something shifts in her expression. Recognition, maybe.*
> 
> "Sent by who?"

Player: Quiet Voice.

> *The tension drops by half. But only half.*
> 
> "Then we're on the same side."
> 
> *She pulls a small package from inside her jacket.*
> 
> "This what you're looking for?"

Player: That's mine?

> "Depends. What's the word?"

Player: I don't know any word.

> *She puts the package back.*
> 
> "Then no. It's not yours."
> 
> "Tell Quiet Voice she should brief her people better."
> 
> *Starts walking away.*

Player: Wait—

> *She doesn't stop.*
> 
> "Find me when you know the word. Or don't. Either way."
> 
> *Gone around the corner.*

The player failed. But they learned something: there's a password. Now they have to go back to the recruiter and ask.

That's a real interaction. That's a story unfolding.
